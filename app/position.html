Plain-English guide

Where does a fixed mortgage rate actually come from?

Your lender borrows the money it lends you. To offer you a fixed rate for two years it locks in its own cost of money for two years, in what's called the swap market. Then it adds a margin to cover its costs and profit. So a fixed mortgage rate is the market rate plus the lender's margin. The market rate changes every day; the margin barely moves.

What does "what the market expects" mean?

If you know what it costs to lock in money for two years, and what it costs to lock in for four years, you can work out what the market is implying for the two years in between. That's arithmetic, not opinion. When this page needs to know what a 2-year deal will cost in two years' time, it uses that number plus the usual lender margin. It's the same number a bank would use. It isn't a forecast, and it can be wrong. The "what if" slider shows how wrong it would have to be to change the answer.

What is "today's money"?

£100 in five years' time is worth less than £100 today, because you could use £100 today to pay down your mortgage and save interest on it. So before adding up payments made in different years, we shrink the later ones to what they're worth today, using your mortgage rate. Without this, a route that pushes cost into the future would look artificially cheap.

Why count what I'd still owe at the end?

Different routes pay off the loan at different speeds. A lower rate pays off more of the balance each month. If we only counted payments, a route could look cheap simply by leaving you owing more at the end. Counting what's still owed makes the comparison fair.

What's the free "lock early" option?

Most lenders let you arrange your next deal up to six months before your current one ends. If a cheaper deal appears before your new one starts, you can switch to it. Since the 2023 Mortgage Charter this is standard at the big lenders. So you never lose by locking early. On Bank of England data since 2009, doing this saved about a quarter of a percent on the rate on average, and saved something four times out of five. We count 0.20% after allowing for a bit of friction, but only on deals you haven't taken yet, never on one you already hold.

What's an early repayment charge?

A penalty for leaving a fixed deal early: a percentage of your balance, usually stepping down each year, for example 5%, 4%, 3%, 2%, 1%. It doesn't change when interest rates move, so when rates fall a lot, leaving can be cheap compared with the saving. This page works out whether it is.

Why does "fee added to the loan" matter?

Most people add the product fee to the mortgage rather than paying it upfront. It's convenient, but you then pay interest on it for the rest of the term, so a £999 fee costs more than £999. The page includes that. Untick the box to see the difference.

What's the standard variable rate?

What your lender moves you onto if you do nothing when your deal ends. It's usually far higher than any fixed deal on offer. The "if you do nothing" figure shows what that costs.

What this page does not do

It doesn't predict interest rates. If you believe rates will fall further than the market expects, the slider tells you how much further they'd need to fall for the shorter deal to win. It doesn't know your circumstances. And it uses typical rates for your loan-to-value unless you type in the ones you've actually been offered, which you should.
